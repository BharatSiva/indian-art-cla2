Kolam & Rangoli

Traditional Indian Floor Art: welcoming the divine, one pattern at a time

Every morning across India, doorsteps bloom with patterns drawn by hand on the ground. Kolam and Rangoli are ancient floor arts that decorate homes, welcome guests and gods, and carry centuries of faith, mathematics and community tradition.

Kolam

Tamil Nadu & South India · drawn daily at dawn
  • Drawn with white rice flour (or rock powder) let fall between the thumb and fingers.
  • Based on a grid of dots (pulli); lines loop around the dots in one continuous path (sikku kolam).
  • Mostly white, symmetrical and geometric; coloured on festivals.
  • Made by women at sunrise after washing the threshold with water or cow-dung water.

Rangoli

Maharashtra, Gujarat & all of India · made on festivals
  • Made with coloured powders, rice, lentils, sand, flower petals or chalk.
  • Mostly freehand; designs include lotuses, peacocks, diyas, deities and mandalas.
  • Bright and multicoloured, often filled edge to edge.
  • Especially made for Diwali, weddings and other celebrations.

Why We Draw Them

Cultural Meaning

  • A sign of a welcoming, happy home; no kolam can signal mourning.
  • Passed from mother to daughter, keeping skills and memory alive.
  • Rice flour feeds ants and birds: daily charity to all living beings.
  • Brings neighbours together, especially in festival contests.

Religious Meaning

  • Invites Goddess Lakshmi, bringing prosperity and good fortune.
  • Marks the threshold as sacred and keeps away evil and negativity.
  • Drawn around altars, tulsi plants and puja spaces.
  • Patterns like the lotus, sun and conch are holy symbols.

Festivals

  • Pongal / Sankranti: giant coloured kolams with pots and sugarcane.
  • Diwali: rangoli with diyas and Lakshmi's footprints.
  • Onam: pookalam made of flower petals in Kerala.
  • Margazhi month: daily kolam competitions in Tamil Nadu.

Common Motifs & Their Meaning

Did you know? Kolam is a form of living mathematics. A sikku kolam is drawn as a single closed line that loops around every dot without lifting the hand, the same idea as an Eulerian path in graph theory. Computer scientists have even studied kolams to understand symmetry, fractals and picture languages.
